About Me

Thank you for visiting my website!

I am a fifth-year Ph.D. candidate in the Department of Economics at the University of California, San Diego. I am an economist who works on applied real-world questions. I combine machine learning, text analysis, and systematic qualitative coding to create indicators and datasets that otherwise do not exist and impose analytical structure on ambiguous problems.

At UC San Diego, my research won the Walter Heller Memorial Prize for best third-year paper. I also passed my qualifying examination with "High Pass" distinction and was awarded the prestigious Dean Koenigsberger Fellowship.

Before joining UC San Diego, I spent two and a half years at the Inter-American Development Bank in Washington, DC, conducting research on macroeconomic and fiscal issues with policy relevance.

I am originally from Chile, and I hold a Master's in Economic Analysis and a B.S. in Economics from the University of Chile. I completed both degrees with Maximum Distinction and with the highest performance in my entering cohort.

Research

Doctoral Research

  • Cleavages, Political Parties, and Polarization (Job Market Paper, draft available).
    Abstract

    Why do some societies become more politically polarized and susceptible to populism? In this paper, I explore the hypothesis that these processes depend on how political parties respond to recent drivers within patterns of political competition that developed much earlier. Foundational political science theories argued that the dimensions along which political parties compete often originate in major conflicts that occurred in the period leading up to the emergence of mass politics. The societal divisions triggered by these conflicts became politically organized and shaped the creation of the modern party system, influencing political competition over the long run. I focus on the Carlist Wars (1833-1876) in Spain as an example of such conflicts and estimate their political effects using a novel dataset spanning three centuries and an instrumental variable exploiting variation in wartime logistical conditions. I find that areas more exposed to the wars exhibit lower political polarization today and weaker support for parties using populist rhetoric. Evidence on mechanisms suggests that the Carlist Wars helped form an additional axis of political competition that, insofar as it does not overlap strongly with the left-right divide, reduces incentives to intensify political polarization and weakens the conditions populist leaders often exploit. The findings remain after including a rich set of controls, and are not explained by the leading alternative hypotheses. Violations of the identification assumptions would need to account for over 60% of the reduced-form effect, while placebo tests provide no evidence that the empirical strategy captures confounding factors. The results shed light on the role of historically rooted structures of political competition in shaping societies' susceptibility to polarization and populism.
